Therapeutic Areas

Ischemic diseases of the heart and brain are among the leading causes of death and disability worldwide. When tissue loses its oxygen supply, damage starts within minutes and continues after blood flow returns.

Acute Ischemic Stroke

An acute ischemic stroke happens when a blood clot blocks an artery that supplies the brain, starving the surrounding brain tissue of oxygen. Nearly 12 million people around the globe have a new stroke every year, and stroke is the second leading cause of death and the third leading cause of death and disability combined, with an estimated global cost of more than US$890 billion a year (World Stroke Organization, 2025).

Acute ischemic stroke is treated by restoring blood flow as quickly as possible, either with clot-dissolving drugs (thrombolysis) or by removing the clot with a catheter (mechanical thrombectomy).

For some patients these treatments work, but they are limited by strict eligibility criteria and narrow time windows, and they do not directly protect the brain. Even with thrombectomy, only around 46% of patients with a large-vessel occlusion regain functional independence at 90 days (The Lancet, 2016). Brain injury can keep spreading after blood flow returns, driven by inflammation, oxidative stress and a leaking blood–brain barrier, and no approved therapy in the U.S. or Europe targets this damage. This leaves a large unmet need for treatments that protect brain tissue alongside reperfusion.

About our stroke clinical development program

We are investigating IV DMT, our intravenous formulation of N,N-dimethyltryptamine, as an add-on to acute stroke care. A Phase 1 study has been completed, and a randomized Phase 2a study in acute ischemic stroke is ready to launch. The same six-hour regimen may also apply to traumatic brain injury.

Myocardial Infarction

A myocardial infarction, or heart attack, occurs when a coronary artery is blocked and part of the heart muscle is deprived of oxygen. The damage is irreversible and can lead to heart failure and other long-term complications. Someone in the United States has a heart attack approximately every 40 seconds (American Heart Association, 2025), and ischemic diseases of the heart and brain together account for about 26% of all deaths worldwide (World Health Organization).

A heart attack is treated by reopening the blocked artery, most often with a stent through percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). More than 1 million PCI procedures are performed in the U.S. each year, followed by long-term medicines such as antiplatelets, beta-blockers and statins.

Restoring blood flow is essential, but reperfusion itself damages heart muscle through oxidative stress, calcium overload and inflammation. In experimental models, this reperfusion injury may account for up to 50% of the final infarct size (The Journal of Clinical Investigation, 2013). Infarct expansion and life-threatening arrhythmias persist despite timely PCI, and there is still no approved therapy that prevents reperfusion injury. With U.S. heart disease costs projected to reach about $1.1 trillion a year by 2035 (American Heart Association), there is a major unmet need for treatments that protect the heart during reperfusion.

About our myocardial infarction program

We are investigating DMT given intravenously during or immediately after a heart attack, including alongside PCI. In a rat model of myocardial ischemia–reperfusion, a single IV dose reduced infarct size from about 23% to under 8% of the area at risk, a reduction of about 65%, and lowered the incidence of ventricular arrhythmias. These preclinical data are unpublished.

Healthy Lifespan

Aging is the strongest single risk factor for ischemic disease of the heart and brain. The world is aging quickly: by 2030, one in six people will be 60 or older, and by 2050 this population will double to 2.1 billion (World Health Organization).

Medicine today treats age-related diseases one at a time, after they appear. There are no approved therapies that target the underlying biology of aging.

Many of the processes that drive ischemic injury, including oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction and chronic inflammation, also drive aging. Therapies that make cells more resilient to these stresses could help people stay healthy for longer.

About our longevity research

In early research, we are exploring whether DMT's effects on cellular stress pathways extend to lifespan. In a preliminary study in the nematode C. elegans, DMT increased median lifespan in a dose-dependent manner, from about 11 to 16 days at the higher dose. This work is at the research stage and will need validation in mammalian models.
